**A ratio compares two amounts. A unit rate tells you how much for one.**

Read this first (about **8 minutes**).

## Ratios

A **ratio** compares two amounts. 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of water is written **2 : 3** ("2 to 3").

The order matters: flour first, water second.

## Equivalent ratios: multiply both sides

Doubling a recipe means **both** amounts double. 2 : 3 becomes 4 : 6, and tripling makes it 6 : 9. They all taste the same.

**Example:** a recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of water. You have 10 cups of flour. How much water?

1. How many times bigger? 10 ÷ 2 = **5 times**.
2. Do the same to water: 3 × 5 = **15 cups**.

## Unit rate: how much for one

A **unit rate** is the amount for **1**. Divide the total by how many.

**Example:** 4 notebooks cost $12. One costs $12 ÷ 4 = **$3**.

Unit rates find the better buy. 6 apples for $3 is $0.50 each. 10 apples for $4 is $0.40 each, so the 10-pack is cheaper per apple.

## Sharing in a ratio

**Example:** split $40 in the ratio 3 : 5.

1. Count the parts: 3 + 5 = **8 parts**.
2. One part: $40 ÷ 8 = **$5**.
3. The shares: 3 × $5 = **$15** and 5 × $5 = **$25**. Check: 15 + 25 = 40.

| Question | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Cost of 1? | total ÷ how many |
| Scale a recipe | find "how many times," then multiply the other side |
| Split in a ratio | add the parts, divide, multiply |

## World — elections and who gets to vote

Reading time: about **11 minutes**. Every World question on the exam comes from here.

Yesterday, Tuesday November 3, was **Election Day**. This was a **midterm**: it happens halfway through a president's four-year term. Every one of the **435** seats in the **House of Representatives** was on the ballot (House terms are **2 years**), plus about a third of the Senate.

### How the president is chosen

Americans don't elect the president directly. Each state gets **electoral votes**, roughly by population. There are **538** in total, and a candidate needs **270** to win.

Most states vote the same way every time. So campaigns spend their money and visits on a few **swing states**, where either party could win. A voter in a swing state gets far more attention than a voter elsewhere.

### The Senate

Every state gets **2 senators**, no matter its size. Wyoming has about 580,000 people; California has about 39 million. Both get 2. So a voter in a small state has **more say in the Senate** than a voter in a big state.

### The fight to vote

At first, in most states only white men who owned property could vote. Every expansion since was **won by people who organized for it**.

- **1870, 15th Amendment:** voting can't be denied because of race. Many Southern states then used **poll taxes** (a fee to vote) and **literacy tests** to keep Black citizens, and poor people, from voting anyway.
- **1920, 19th Amendment:** women win the vote, after decades of marching, petitioning, and going to jail.
- **1964, 24th Amendment:** bans poll taxes in federal elections.
- **1965, Voting Rights Act:** after the marches from Selma, Alabama, it outlaws literacy tests and sends federal officials to protect voters. Black voter registration in the South rose sharply.
- **1971, 26th Amendment:** lowers the voting age from 21 to **18**. The argument: if 18-year-olds can be drafted to fight in Vietnam, they should be able to vote.

### The secret ballot

You vote alone, and no one sees your choice. That way, **no boss, landlord, or official can pressure or punish you** for how you voted. Before secret ballots, they often did.

### Still argued about today

Voter ID laws, how many polling places a neighborhood gets, mail-in voting, and whether people with past prison sentences can vote. Ask of any rule: **does it make it easier or harder for eligible people to vote, and for whom?**
