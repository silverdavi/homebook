# Thursday, October 22 — Day 35

**Density tells you how tightly matter is packed.**

This is a new subject. The first block teaches it before any independent work. This reading takes about **8 minutes**.

## Start with one rule

**density = mass ÷ volume**

- **mass** is how much matter, measured here in grams
- **volume** is how much space, measured here in cm³
- **density** is the mass packed into each cm³

## Find density

A block has a mass of 24 g and a volume of 6 cm³.

24 ÷ 6 = **4 g/cm³**

Read that answer as “4 grams in each cubic centimeter.”

## Find mass

After density is comfortable, use:

**mass = density × volume**

If density is 4 g/cm³ and volume is 5 cm³:

4 × 5 = **20 g**

Today does not ask you to rearrange all 3 formulas at once. Practice density first, then mass. The exam uses those same two forms.

## Extra — if you finished early (optional)

Nothing here is on the exam.

- **Why the unit is g/cm³:** you divided grams by cubic centimeters, so the unit is "grams **per** cubic centimeter." The unit tells you exactly what you did. Monday is all about this.
- **Water is 1 g/cm³.** Anything with a smaller density floats; anything bigger sinks. Ice is about **0.92 g/cm³**, which is why ice cubes float and why lakes freeze from the top down, so fish survive the winter underneath.
- **Gold is about 19 g/cm³.** A gold bar the size of a phone would weigh about 1.5 kg, as much as a big bottle of water.
- **Try it:** guess whether an apple, a coin, and a pencil float, then test them in a bowl of water.

## World — flags, borders, and people on the move

Reading time: about **12 minutes**. Every World question on the exam comes from here.

### Four flags and what they mean

A flag is a country's story compressed into a few colors.

- **Ukraine (Eastern Europe):** two stripes, **blue over yellow**. People often read it as blue sky above golden wheat fields; Ukraine is one of the world's big grain growers.
- **Israel (Middle East, on the Mediterranean):** white with two blue stripes and a blue **Star of David** in the center. The stripes recall the tallit, the Jewish prayer shawl. Adopted in 1948.
- **Palestine (West Bank and Gaza):** **black, white, and green stripes with a red triangle** at the side. The design comes from the flag of the 1916 Arab Revolt against Ottoman rule.
- **South Africa (southern tip of Africa):** six colors and a **sideways green Y**. It was adopted in 1994, when apartheid (legal racial segregation) ended and Nelson Mandela became president. The Y shows different paths coming together into one.

### State vs. nation: not the same thing

- A **state** is a territory with a government and political authority: it has borders, laws, and a capital.
- A **nation** is a community of people connected by identity, culture, language, or history.

Sometimes they match closely; Iceland is one example. Often they don't:

- The **Kurds**, about 30–40 million people, are a nation spread across Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria, with no state of their own.
- **Palestinians** are a nation. Most UN member countries recognize a State of Palestine, but it does not control its own borders.

### Borders move

Borders look permanent on a map, but **empire, colonization, war, and negotiation** have redrawn them again and again.

- At the **Berlin Conference** of 1884–85, European powers divided much of Africa among themselves. No Africans were invited, and many new lines cut straight through communities that had existed long before.
- In **1947**, British India was split into India and Pakistan. Around **15 million people** were forced to move, and hundreds of thousands were killed.

A border can divide families, farms, and languages that existed long before the line was drawn.

### Why people migrate

Humans have always moved: for **family, work, education, safety, and climate**. Migration rarely has one cause.

- From about 1910 to 1970, around **6 million Black Americans** left the segregated South for northern and western cities. This is called the Great Migration.
- Today, more than **100 million people** worldwide have been forced from their homes.

Two legal words:

- A **refugee** has **crossed a border after being forced to flee persecution, war, or violence**.
- An **asylum seeker** is asking another country for **legal protection** and is waiting for a decision.

### Whose story gets told?

Government records list names, dates, and ports. They rarely say why someone left, what the journey was like, or what it felt like to arrive. **Migrants' own accounts** fill that gap. Good history uses both kinds of source.
