**Two numbers can find any point: (x, y).**

Start with the “Find any point” animation above (about **5 minutes**). Then read this (about **6 minutes**).

## Across, then up

A point is written **(x, y)**.

- **x** comes first: how far **right (+)** or **left (−)** from the center.
- **y** comes second: how far **up (+)** or **down (−)**.

(3, 2) means 3 right, 2 up. (−4, −3) means 4 left, 3 down. The center (0, 0) is called the **origin**.

## The four quadrants

The two axes cut the grid into 4 **quadrants**, numbered counterclockwise from the top right:

| Quadrant | Signs | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 (I) | (+, +) | (3, 4) |
| 2 (II) | (−, +) | (−2, 5) |
| 3 (III) | (−, −) | (−4, −1) |
| 4 (IV) | (+, −) | (5, −3) |

## Moving a point

Move left or right: **only x changes**. Move up or down: **only y changes**.

Start at (2, 3) and move 5 left: x becomes 2 − 5 = **−3**. The point is now (−3, 3).

## Distance along a line

If two points share the same y (or the same x), count the gap in the other number. This is Tuesday's number line again:

(−2, 4) to (5, 4): from −2 to 0 is 2, from 0 to 5 is 5, so **7 units**.

## World — latitude, longitude, and time zones

Reading time: about **11 minutes**. Every World question on the exam comes from here.

### Coordinates for the whole planet

Latitude and longitude are an (x, y) grid wrapped around a ball.

- **Latitude** measures **how far north or south of the equator** a place is. The **equator** is **0° latitude**; the poles are 90° N and 90° S.
- **Longitude** measures how far east or west. Its lines run **between the North and South Poles**.
- Everything south of the equator is the **Southern Hemisphere**, including all of **Australia**.

### Who gets to be zero?

The equator is a natural zero; it's halfway between the poles. But there is no natural zero for longitude. Someone had to choose.

At a conference in Washington in **1884**, most countries voted for the line through the Royal Observatory in **Greenwich, London**, as the **Prime Meridian (0°)**. Why? **Britain's navy and empire already used it on most of the world's sea charts.** Power decided the starting line. France refused and kept using Paris for decades.

### Time zones

Before the 1880s, every town used **local sun time**: noon was when the sun was highest. So nearby towns had slightly different times.

That was chaos for **railroads**, which needed consistent schedules. In **1883**, US railroads adopted **standard time zones**.

The math: Earth turns **360° in 24 hours**, so each hour of turning is 360 ÷ 24 = **15° of longitude**. That's why there are **24** main time zones, each roughly **15°** wide.

Time zones are political too:

- **China** spans about 5 geographic time zones but uses **1 official time** (Beijing time). In far-western Xinjiang, the sun can rise around 10 a.m.
- The **International Date Line**, near **180° longitude** in the Pacific, is where the calendar date changes. Kiribati moved it in 1995 so all its islands share the same day.
