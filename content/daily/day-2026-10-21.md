## Perimeter means around

Perimeter is the distance around the outside edge.

For a rectangle that is 4 cm long and 3 cm wide, add all 4 sides:

**4 + 3 + 4 + 3 = 14 cm**

You can also use **2 × length + 2 × width**. It means exactly the same thing.

## Area means inside

Area counts how many square units cover the inside.

A 4-by-3 rectangle has 4 columns and 3 rows:

**4 × 3 = 12 cm²**

Area is **length × width**. It is not length divided by width.

## Keep the questions separate

For a 5 cm by 2 cm rectangle:

- perimeter: 5 + 2 + 5 + 2 = **14 cm**
- area: 5 × 2 = **10 cm²**

Ordinary **cm** means a line or distance. **cm²** means squares covering a surface.

Today has only rectangles. There are no triangle or volume questions. The exam uses only the same two ideas taught and practiced here.

## World — biomes: Earth's big neighborhoods

Reading time: about **12 minutes**. Read this before the World sheet; every World question on the exam comes from here.

A **biome** is a huge region where the climate (mostly temperature and rain) decides what can grow, and the plants decide which animals can live there. Four big ones:

### 1. Tundra — cold and treeless

- **Where:** the far north of Canada, Alaska, Greenland, Siberia (Russia), and northern Scandinavia, plus high mountaintops.
- **Climate:** long, dark, freezing winters; summers of only 6–10 weeks.
- **Why no trees:** the ground underneath is **permafrost**: ground that stays frozen for **at least 2 years in a row**, often for thousands of years. Roots can't go deep, so only moss, lichen, and low shrubs grow.
- **Life:** caribou, Arctic foxes, snowy owls, and millions of birds that fly in for the short summer.
- **Today:** the Arctic is warming about **3–4 times faster** than the rest of the planet. As permafrost thaws, the ground slumps, roads and houses crack, and old frozen plant matter releases more greenhouse gas.

### 2. Desert — defined by dry, not by hot

- **Where:** the Sahara (North Africa), the Arabian Desert, the Atacama (Chile), the Gobi (Mongolia and China), and Antarctica.
- **The key rule:** a desert is defined by **very low precipitation** (under about 25 cm of rain or snow a year), **not** by heat.
- **Surprise:** Antarctica is the largest desert on Earth. It is freezing *and* a desert, because almost no new snow falls there.
- **Life:** deserts are not empty. Camels, cacti, scorpions, and desert foxes are all adapted to save water.

### 3. Rainforest — warm, wet, and crowded with life

- **Where:** near the equator: the Amazon (Brazil, Peru, Colombia), the Congo Basin (central Africa), and Indonesia.
- **Climate:** hot all year and rain almost every day, often over 200 cm a year.
- **Biodiversity** means the number of different kinds of living things. Rainforests cover only about 6% of land but hold **about half** of all land species.
- **Today:** cutting forest for cattle ranches, soy, and palm oil is the biggest threat.

### 4. Grassland — grasses instead of forest

- **Where:** the North American prairies, the Argentine pampas, the steppes of Ukraine and Kazakhstan, and the African savanna (a grassland with scattered trees).
- **Climate:** a dry season and a wet season. There is enough rain for grass, but not enough for a thick forest.
- **What keeps grasslands open:** **fire and grazing**. Bison, wildebeest, and zebra eat the grass, and fires (natural ones and ones set on purpose by people) burn young trees, while grass regrows from its roots. People have done this for **thousands of years**.

### Biomes blend; they don't have walls

On a map, biomes look like colored blocks with sharp edges. Real ones fade into each other: forest thins into savanna, and savanna dries into desert. And almost every biome has been shaped by people for thousands of years.

## Climate justice: who caused it, and who pays

**Climate justice** asks a fairness question: **who caused climate change, who is harmed by it, and who has the money and power to respond?**

Example: in 2022, floods covered about **one-third of Pakistan** and displaced millions of people. Pakistan has produced **less than 1%** of the world's greenhouse gases. Rich, industrialized countries produced most of them and have more money to build defenses.

The people **most exposed** are often those who did the least to cause it: poorer countries, small island nations threatened by rising seas, farmers who depend on rain, and poor neighborhoods inside rich countries.

## Indigenous peoples and the land

Long before governments created national parks, **Indigenous communities managed these landscapes**. Aboriginal Australians used small, careful "cool burns" to prevent huge wildfires. Native nations in North America used fire to keep prairies open for bison. Amazon peoples planted and tended forests.

Conservation has a dark side too. When **Yellowstone**, the first US national park, was created in 1872, Native peoples such as the Shoshone were later pushed out. In East Africa, Maasai families have been evicted to make room for parks and safari tourism. Protecting nature by **removing the people who had cared for it** caused real harm.

Today, studies show that land where Indigenous peoples hold **legal land rights** often loses less forest than nearby land. Respecting their rights and knowledge is fair, and it also tends to protect nature better.
