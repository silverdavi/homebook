# Thursday, October 29 — Day 38

**A map is the real world shrunk by one number: the scale.**

Start with the “Map scale” animation above (about **5 minutes**). Then read this (about **6 minutes**).

## Map to real: multiply

A map says **1 cm = 10 km**. Two towns are 3 cm apart on the map.

**3 cm × 10 km per cm = 30 km**

The cm cancel, just like Monday, and km is left.

## Real to map: divide by the scale

Two cities are 40 km apart. How far apart on the same map?

**40 km ÷ 10 km per cm = 4 cm**

You wanted cm, the bottom of "km per cm," so you divide by the rate.

## Find the scale: divide

On a map, 4 cm stands for 20 km. How much is 1 cm?

**20 km ÷ 4 cm = 5 km per cm**

"Per cm" means divide.

| You know… | You want… | Do this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| map cm and the scale | real km | **multiply** |
| real km and the scale | map cm | **divide by the scale** |
| map cm and real km | the scale | **km ÷ cm** |

## Extra — only if you finished early (optional)

- **Scale as a ratio:** 1 : 100,000 means 1 cm on the map is 100,000 cm in real life, which is **1 km**. Hiking maps often use 1 : 25,000.
- **Measure your world:** find a map with a scale bar (any atlas, or the scale bar on a paper map). Measure from your town to a city you know, then convert.
- **Models:** a 1 : 24 model car is 24 times smaller in every direction. If the model is 20 cm long, the real car is 20 × 24 = 480 cm, almost 5 m.

## World — how maps lie (a little), and why it matters

Reading time: about **12 minutes**. Every World question on the exam comes from here.

### You can't flatten an orange peel

Earth is round. A map is flat. A **map projection** is a method for turning the globe into a flat map, and **every projection distorts something**: shapes, sizes, distances, or directions. Mapmakers choose what to sacrifice.

### Mercator: great for sailors, bad for sizes

In **1569**, Gerardus Mercator made a map where any straight line is a **constant compass direction**, which was perfect for steering a ship across an ocean. But to do it he stretched everything far from the equator.

- On a Mercator map, **Greenland looks about as big as Africa**. In reality, **Africa is about 14 times larger**.
- **Russia, Canada, and Europe look huge**; places near the equator look small.
- **Africa** is so big that the **US, China, and India fit inside it** with room to spare.

Because Mercator maps hung in classrooms for centuries, many people grew up with a picture of the world where Europe and North America looked bigger and more central than they are.

### Equal-area maps

In the **1970s**, Arno Peters promoted the **Gall–Peters** map, which shows land areas at their **true relative size**, so Africa and South America look as large as they really are. Its shapes look stretched, but the sizes are fair. Today, many geographers prefer compromise maps like the **Robinson** or **Equal Earth** projection.

**An equal-area map keeps the relative sizes of places correct.**

### North at the top is a choice

Space has no up or down. **Putting north at the top is a convention** that mapmakers chose. Many medieval European maps put east at the top, and early Islamic maps often put south at the top. Australians sometimes sell "upside-down" maps as a joke that makes a real point.

### The big pieces

- **7 continents**, the way they are usually counted in English-speaking schools: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, South America.
- **Asia** is the largest continent; **Africa** is second.
- **5 oceans:** the **Pacific** (the largest, bigger than all land combined), Atlantic, **Indian** (between Africa and Australia), Southern, and Arctic.
